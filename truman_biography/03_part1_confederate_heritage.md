# Part I: Confederate Heritage

## The Slaveholding Families (1840s-1884)

---

### Chapter Summary

Harry S. Truman was born into families that had owned enslaved persons, fought for the Confederacy, and suffered at Union hands during the Civil War. This heritage shaped a "violently unreconstructed" worldview that Truman absorbed from childhood and carried, in modified form, throughout his life. Understanding this background is essential to comprehending both his early racism and the remarkable—if incomplete—transformation he later underwent.

---

### The Migration from Kentucky

In the 1840s, two Kentucky families—the Youngs and the Trumans—joined the great westward migration to Missouri's frontier.[^1] They were, as biographer David McCullough describes them, "of the yeoman gentry of the upper South," families that "had fought Indians, staked out lands, raised crops, acquired a few slaves, and prospered."[^2]

All four of Harry Truman's grandparents were born in Kentucky.[^3] When they crossed into Missouri, they brought their human property with them. The future president would later be described, accurately, as "a son of a slave State, the grandson of slaveowners."[^4]

**Claim C-01-001:** All four grandparents migrated from Kentucky to Missouri in the 1840s with enslaved persons.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Truman Library Genealogy; McCullough, *Truman*

The Trumans and Youngs settled near Westport, Missouri, on what was then the American frontier. Solomon Young, Harry's maternal grandfather, became a successful farmer and freighter who ran Conestoga wagon trains along the Overland Trail. He acquired substantial land holdings and, like his neighbors, relied on enslaved labor.[^5]

---

### Confederate Sympathies and Civil War Trauma

When the Civil War erupted, the Trumans and Youngs faced an agonizing situation. Missouri was a border state where neighbors killed neighbors and guerrilla warfare made ordinary life impossible. The families "sympathized with the South, more out of ties of kinship and culture than personal economic considerations."[^6]

**Claim C-01-002:** Family sympathized with South due to "kinship and culture."
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** McCullough, *Truman*

The western Missouri border became one of the war's most vicious theaters. Pro-Union "Jayhawkers" from Kansas raided Missouri farms; pro-Confederate "Bushwhackers" retaliated. The Quantrill Raiders—Confederate guerrillas notorious for the Lawrence Massacre—held annual reunions in Independence, Missouri, where Harry Truman grew up, well into the twentieth century.[^7]

The Young family suffered directly at Union hands, an experience that left permanent scars on the family's collective memory. In 1861, when Harry's grandmother Harriet Louisa Young was tending the farm alone (her husband Solomon was away on a freighting expedition), a band of Union-supporting Jayhawkers descended on the property.[^8]

The raiders forced Harriet to cook for them until her hands blistered. They slaughtered livestock, burned barns, and stole the family silver. Most horrifically, according to family accounts, they "repeatedly hanged her son Harrison, a boy of thirteen, by the neck" in an attempt to extract information about his father's whereabouts and any hidden valuables.[^9]

**Claim C-01-003:** Jayhawkers raided Young farm, tortured the family.
**Confidence:** PROBABLE (family oral tradition)
**Sources:** McCullough, *Truman*; Truman Library family history

The eldest Young son, William, served as a Confederate soldier.[^10] When the war ended, the family had lost much of what they had built—but their bitterness toward the Union would prove more durable than any material possession.

---

### Martha Ellen Young: "Violently Unreconstructed"

Harry Truman's mother, Martha Ellen Young Truman (1852-1947), was nine years old when the Jayhawkers attacked her family's farm. She witnessed the humiliation of her mother and the torture of her brother. For the remaining eighty-six years of her life, she never forgave the winning side.

Historians consistently describe Martha Ellen as "violently unreconstructed" regarding the Civil War—a characterization that appears across multiple independent sources.[^11] Her resentment was not an abstract political position but a visceral hatred rooted in personal trauma.

**Claim C-01-004:** Martha Ellen Truman was "violently unreconstructed."
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Leuchtenburg, "Conversion"; McCullough, *Truman*; multiple accounts

The depth of Martha Ellen's Confederate loyalties became legendary. A widely repeated story holds that when she first visited the White House in 1945 after her son became president, she refused to sleep in the Lincoln Bedroom.[^12] The Truman family denied this specific account, but the fact that it was universally believed speaks to her well-known attitudes.

Harry's grandmother Harriet was equally unreconstructed. When young Harry Truman joined the Missouri National Guard before World War I and visited her in his blue uniform, she ordered him out of her house. "Harry, that's the first time a uniform of that color has been in this house since the Civil War," she reportedly said. "Don't you bring it back."[^13]

---

### The Lessons Harry Learned

Harry Truman, born May 8, 1884, in Lamar, Missouri, absorbed these attitudes from infancy. The historian William Leuchtenburg summarizes the education Harry received:

> Truman literally learned at his mother's knee to share the South's view of the War between the States. He grew up detesting the meddlesome abolitionists, decried the racial experimentation of Reconstruction, and sneered at Thaddeus Stevens, that "crippled moron." He also acquired an abiding belief in white supremacy.[^14]

**Claim C-02-004:** Truman learned white supremacy at his mother's knee.
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** Leuchtenburg, "The Conversion of Harry Truman"

Truman's parents were, in his own recollection, "a violently unreconstructed southern family" and "Lincoln haters."[^15] Independence, Missouri, where Harry grew up from age six, was "southern in its atmosphere" and "still vividly mindful of three separate Civil War battles."[^16]

The town's Confederate heritage was not merely historical memory but active identity. Harry Truman took pride in this heritage. He was a direct descendant of two Confederate soldiers. As an adult, he became a dues-paying member of the Sons of Confederate Veterans—an organization that maintained and celebrated Confederate memory.[^17]

### Historical Context: Missouri and Race

To understand Truman's formation, it is essential to understand Missouri's peculiar position in the American racial order.

Missouri was a slave state that never joined the Confederacy, but it never experienced Reconstruction either. Unlike the Deep South, Missouri did not disenfranchise its Black citizens after the war—but neither did it integrate them. The state developed its own system of segregation, less legally formalized than Jim Crow in the former Confederacy but no less real in practice.[^18]

Black Missourians occupied a liminal space: they could vote (and would become important to urban Democratic machines like Kansas City's Pendergast organization), but they lived under customary segregation in housing, education, and public accommodations. This was the racial order Harry Truman knew from birth—one where Black people existed as a subordinate caste, where racist attitudes were the norm among whites of all classes, and where the Lost Cause narrative of the Civil War was accepted truth.

---

### What This Heritage Meant

The Confederate heritage Truman inherited shaped him in ways both obvious and subtle:

1. **Racial hierarchy as natural order:** Truman grew up in a world where white supremacy was assumed, not argued. Black inferiority was simply how things were.

2. **Resentment toward federal intervention:** The family's Civil War trauma created deep suspicion of federal power used for racial purposes. Reconstruction was remembered as tyranny.

3. **The Lost Cause narrative:** Truman learned to see the Civil War as Northern aggression against Southern culture, not as a struggle over slavery.

4. **Personal loyalty as supreme value:** The family's suffering taught that you stood by your own—your family, your community, your comrades—against outside interference.

These attitudes would persist in Truman for decades, expressed in private letters that documented his prejudices with uncomfortable clarity. Yet this same heritage contained seeds of what would later enable his civil rights actions: a fierce sense of honor, a belief that a man's word meant something, and a constitutional patriotism that eventually transcended his regional loyalties.

The grandson of slaveholders would become the president who desegregated the military. But first, he would spend forty years expressing views his Confederate ancestors would have recognized and approved.

### Notes on Evidence and Uncertainty

**Well-documented claims:**
- Family migration from Kentucky with enslaved persons
- Confederate sympathies during Civil War
- Martha Ellen Truman's "unreconstructed" attitudes
- Harry's membership in Sons of Confederate Veterans

**Claims based on family tradition:**
- Specific details of Jayhawker raid (repeated hanging of Harrison Young)
- The "Lincoln Bedroom" refusal story (denied by family)

**Interpretive claims:**
- The extent to which childhood heritage determined adult attitudes
- The relative weight of "kinship and culture" vs. economics in Confederate sympathies

The documentary record for this period relies heavily on family oral tradition preserved in later accounts and scholarly interpretation. While the broad outlines are well established, specific details of mid-nineteenth-century events should be treated with appropriate caution.
