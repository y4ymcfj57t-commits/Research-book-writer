# Part IV: The Senate Years

## Public Evolution, Private Persistence (1935-1944)

---

### Chapter Summary

Harry Truman's decade in the U.S. Senate (1935-1945) presents a deeply contradictory record on race. His private correspondence continued to include vile racial slurs. Yet his public positions began to shift, at least rhetorically, toward acknowledging Black Americans' claims to equal treatment. Most controversially, Truman participated in a procedural maneuver that helped kill the Costigan-Wagner anti-lynching bill in 1935—a vote that complicates any simple narrative of his evolution. By 1940, however, he was publicly advocating for "the brotherhood of all men before law."

---

### Arrival in Washington: "The Senator from Pendergast"

Truman arrived in Washington in January 1935 with the stigma of machine politics attached. He was widely dismissed as "the Senator from Pendergast"—a hack who owed his seat to a corrupt boss and would do the machine's bidding.[^1]

This reputation was not entirely fair. While Truman owed his nomination to Pendergast, he had proven himself an honest and effective administrator in Jackson County. He would prove himself in the Senate too, eventually chairing the Truman Committee investigating war production waste during World War II—work that brought him national recognition and led to his vice-presidential nomination in 1944.[^2]

But on matters of race, Truman's Senate years reveal a man caught between his heritage and his political requirements, between private prejudice and public positioning.

---

### The Anti-Lynching Vote of 1935

The single most problematic moment in Truman's pre-presidential civil rights record came in his first year as a senator.

The Costigan-Wagner anti-lynching bill would have made lynching a federal crime, imposing penalties on state officials who failed to prevent mob violence. The legislation was desperately needed: between 1882 and 1968, nearly 5,000 people—overwhelmingly Black—were murdered by lynch mobs in America. State authorities routinely refused to prosecute these crimes.[^3]

Southern Democrats vehemently opposed the bill, viewing federal anti-lynching legislation as an invasion of states' rights that threatened the entire Jim Crow system. They mounted a filibuster that paralyzed the Senate for weeks in the spring of 1935.

**Claim C-04-001:** In 1935, Truman voted for a motion to adjourn that effectively killed the Costigan-Wagner anti-lynching bill.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Congressional Record, 1935; Rupkatha Journal article

In late April 1935, Democratic Senate Majority Leader Joseph T. Robinson forced an adjournment motion that would effectively kill the bill by ending the session. The vote failed very narrowly at first. But political trading brought another adjournment motion to pass on May 1, 1935. Truman was among the votes that switched to support adjournment.[^4]

The bill died. It would take another thirty years—until the Civil Rights Act of 1964—for federal civil rights protections remotely comparable to what Costigan-Wagner proposed.

---

### Interpreting the 1935 Vote

How damning is Truman's vote?

**The Damning View:** Truman actively participated in killing legislation that would have protected Black Americans from murder. He sided with Southern segregationists against the most basic civil rights demand of the era. Whatever he later did as president, he helped ensure that federal anti-lynching protection was delayed by decades.

**The Contextualizing View:** Truman was a freshman senator from a border state, dependent on the Democratic leadership for committee assignments and legislative success. The adjournment motion was a procedural vote, not a direct vote against anti-lynching legislation. Many senators voted for procedural motions they personally regretted to maintain party unity.

**The Documentary Reality:** The vote happened. Truman's name is in the Congressional Record. The effect was to kill legislation that could have saved lives. Whatever his personal feelings, his action aligned with Southern segregationists.

Some sources note Truman's "ties with notorious Mississippi demagogue Theodore Bilbo during the 1930s" as further evidence of his alignment with the segregationist bloc.[^5] The full nature of this relationship requires further research, but Truman clearly operated within, rather than against, the Democratic coalition that depended on Southern support.

---

### Private Racism in the Senate Years

While Truman's public positions on race remained largely silent through the mid-1930s, his private correspondence shows unchanged attitudes.

**Claim C-04-003:** In 1937, Senator Truman described White House waiters as "an army of coons" in a letter to his daughter.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Letter at Truman Library; multiple secondary citations

In 1937, writing to his daughter Margaret, Truman described the White House waiters as "an army of coons."[^6] This was not an unusual locution for him—merely the continuation of language he had used since at least 1911.

**Claim C-04-004:** In 1939, Truman referred to "nigger picnic day" in a letter to his wife.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Letter at Truman Library; multiple secondary citations

Two years later, in 1939, he wrote to Bess about "nigger picnic day"—apparently a reference to a Juneteenth celebration or similar event.[^7]

These letters reveal a man whose private self remained largely unchanged from the young farmer who had written to Bess in 1911. Whatever accommodations Truman made to political reality, whatever rhetorical adjustments he offered in public, his personal attitudes—expressed to his closest family members—still relied on dehumanizing language.

**Claim C-04-002:** Truman was "openly racist when among his Senate peers" while lobbying for an end to legalized racial discrimination.
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** King Institute Encyclopedia

The Martin Luther King Jr. Research and Education Institute summarizes the paradox: Truman was "openly racist when among his Senate peers" while simultaneously lobbying "for an end to legalized racial discrimination because it violated basic American ideals."[^8]

This framing captures something essential. Truman's racism was social and personal—expressed in casual conversation, in letters home, in the company of fellow white Southerners. His opposition to discrimination was constitutional and political—rooted in abstract principles about American ideals and practical calculations about electoral coalitions.

The two coexisted without apparent tension, at least in Truman's mind.

---

### The 1940 Campaign: A Rhetorical Shift

Truman faced a difficult reelection campaign in 1940. Tom Pendergast had been convicted of income tax evasion in 1939 and sent to prison. The machine that had launched Truman's career was in ruins.[^9]

Without Pendergast's support, Truman had to build a new coalition. He faced primary challenges from Maurice Milligan (the prosecutor who had convicted Pendergast) and former Governor Lloyd Stark. His political survival required reaching beyond his traditional base.[^10]

Truman's response was to build a broader coalition that included constituencies the Pendergast machine had cultivated: labor unions, women, farmers—and African Americans.

**Claim C-04-005:** In 1940, Truman said "I believe in the brotherhood of man, not merely the brotherhood of white men, but the brotherhood of all men before law."
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** NPS, "Harry S Truman and Civil Rights"

In a campaign speech in Sedalia, Missouri, Truman declared: "I believe in the brotherhood of man, not merely the brotherhood of white men, but the brotherhood of all men before law."[^11]

This was new language for Truman—a public commitment to legal equality that went beyond anything he had said before. Whether it reflected genuine evolution or political calculation (or both), it marked a departure from his silence on racial matters.

Truman actively courted African American support in the 1940 campaign:
- He sought the backing of Dr. William J. Thompkins, the African American physician and Democratic organizer he had known since his Pendergast days.
- He won the endorsement of Chester Arthur Franklin, editor of *The Call*, despite Franklin's Republican affiliation.
- He campaigned on a platform that implicitly recognized Black Missourians as full members of his political coalition.[^12]

The strategy worked. In a tight three-way primary, Stark and Milligan split the anti-Pendergast vote, and Truman won by 8,000 votes. He went on to defeat his Republican opponent 51-49% in November.[^13]

---

### The Truman Committee and Rising Prominence

Reelected in 1940, Truman found his Senate footing. His most significant achievement was chairing the Senate Special Committee to Investigate the National Defense Program (1941-1944), known as the Truman Committee.

This oversight body investigated waste, fraud, and inefficiency in war production. It saved billions of dollars and earned Truman a reputation for integrity and competence. A 1943 *Time* magazine cover story brought him national attention.[^14]

The Truman Committee work was not directly related to civil rights, but it established Truman as a serious figure and positioned him for the vice presidency. It also reinforced his self-image as someone who did the right thing regardless of political pressure—an identity that would prove relevant to his later civil rights actions.

---

### Selection as Vice President

In 1944, Democratic Party leaders grew concerned about President Franklin Roosevelt's declining health and wanted a less controversial vice-presidential candidate than the incumbent Henry Wallace, whom conservatives viewed as too liberal.

Truman was acceptable to multiple party factions: labor liked him, Southerners found him tolerable, city bosses knew him, and he had proven himself competent and honest. FDR acquiesced in his selection.

At the Democratic convention in July 1944, Truman was nominated for vice president. He campaigned with Roosevelt that fall and, on January 20, 1945, was sworn in as vice president.

Eighty-two days later, on April 12, 1945, Franklin Roosevelt died. Harry Truman—the grandson of slaveholders, the man who had written of "niggers" and "coons," the senator who had voted to kill anti-lynching legislation—became President of the United States.

---

### Assessment: The Senate Record

Truman's ten years in the Senate (1935-1945) reveal a man in transition—or at least in repositioning:

**What Changed:**
- Public rhetoric shifted toward acknowledging "the brotherhood of all men before law"
- Electoral strategy incorporated African Americans as a constituency to be courted
- The Pendergast model of practical politics expanded to statewide coalition-building

**What Didn't Change:**
- Private language remained viciously racist through the 1930s
- No significant civil rights legislation was championed
- The 1935 anti-lynching vote aligned with segregationists

**The Overall Picture:**
The Senate years show Truman learning that Black votes mattered and that inclusive rhetoric could be politically useful—while maintaining private attitudes that would have appalled most of his Black constituents. This gap between public positioning and private belief would characterize his presidency as well.

The question facing Truman as he unexpectedly became president was whether political calculation and rhetorical evolution would translate into action—and whether the man who had voted to adjourn debate on anti-lynching legislation would himself become an advocate for civil rights.

The answer, unexpectedly, would be yes. But the catalyst would not be political calculation. It would be violence.
