# Part VI: Executive Actions and Military Desegregation

## Executive Order 9981 and the 1948 Election (1948-1953)

---

### Chapter Summary

When Congress refused to act on his civil rights program, President Truman used executive power to advance integration. On July 26, 1948, he signed Executive Order 9981, ending segregation in the armed forces—an order that repudiated 170 years of official military discrimination. This action, taken eleven days after Southern Democrats walked out of the Democratic convention, helped define the 1948 election. Against all predictions, Truman won—proving that a Democrat could champion civil rights and still capture the presidency. The implementation of military integration, while not completed until 1954, represented the most significant federal civil rights achievement since Reconstruction.

---

### Congressional Rejection

Truman's February 1948 civil rights message to Congress went nowhere. Southern Democrats controlled key committee chairmanships through the seniority system. They threatened filibuster against any civil rights legislation.

The Senate had never in its history voted to end a filibuster on a civil rights bill. Southern senators used this procedural weapon ruthlessly. Anti-lynching legislation, poll tax abolition, FEPC extension—all died by filibuster or the threat of it.[^1]

When Southern senators immediately threatened filibuster after Truman's message, he understood that legislation was impossible. But executive action remained available.

---

### The Democratic Convention of 1948

The 1948 Democratic National Convention in Philadelphia became a battleground over civil rights.

Truman initially proposed a civil rights plank that moderated his earlier strong statements—an attempt to hold the party together. This disappointed Northern liberals who wanted the platform to match the president's message to Congress.[^2]

Minneapolis Mayor Hubert Humphrey, 37 years old and seeking a Senate seat, led the fight for a stronger plank. On July 14, 1948, he delivered what became one of the most consequential speeches in convention history:

**Claim C-07-001:** Humphrey urged Democrats to "get out of the shadow of states' rights and walk forthrightly into the bright sunshine of human rights."
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Convention records; American Rhetoric

> "My friends, to those who say that we are rushing this issue of civil rights, I say to them we are 172 years late... The time has arrived in America for the Democratic Party to get out of the shadow of states' rights and walk forthrightly into the bright sunshine of human rights."[^3]

The convention erupted. When the roll was called, the stronger civil rights plank—the "Biemiller plank" co-sponsored by Humphrey—passed 651.5 to 582.5.[^4]

**Claim C-07-002:** Southern Democrats walked out and formed the States' Rights Democratic Party (Dixiecrats), nominating Strom Thurmond.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Multiple historical accounts

The Southern response was immediate. All 22 Mississippi delegates and 13 Alabama delegates walked out of the convention hall. Within days, they convened in Birmingham, Alabama, to form the States' Rights Democratic Party—the "Dixiecrats"—and nominated South Carolina Governor Strom Thurmond for president.[^5]

The Democratic Party had split over civil rights. Truman was now running against both the Republican nominee Thomas Dewey and a Southern revolt from his own party.

---

### Executive Order 9981

Eleven days after the convention, on July 26, 1948, President Truman signed two executive orders that would reshape American society.

**Claim C-06-006:** On July 26, 1948, Truman issued Executive Order 9981 ending segregation in the armed forces.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** National Archives; Federal Register

Executive Order 9980 established fair employment practices in the federal civilian workforce—requiring that all hiring and promotion decisions be made without regard to race, color, religion, or national origin.[^6]

Executive Order 9981 was more far-reaching. It declared:

> "It is hereby declared to be the policy of the President that there shall be equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed services without regard to race, color, religion or national origin. This policy shall be put into effect as rapidly as possible, having due regard to the time required to effectuate any necessary changes without impairing efficiency or morale."[^7]

When Truman signed this order, he repudiated 170 years of officially sanctioned military discrimination.[^8] Since the Revolution, Black Americans had served in segregated units under white officers, denied equal opportunity for advancement and often relegated to support roles rather than combat.

The order created the President's Committee on Equality of Treatment and Opportunity in the Armed Services (the Fahy Committee) to oversee implementation.[^9]

---

### Military Resistance

The military's response to Executive Order 9981 was not enthusiastic.

**Claim C-07-004:** General Omar Bradley opposed the order, saying "The Army is not out to make any social reforms."
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** NPS; History.com

Army Chief of Staff General Omar Bradley publicly complained: "The Army is not out to make any social reforms. The Army will put men of different races in different companies. It will change that policy when the Nation as a whole changes it." Truman forced Bradley to issue a public apology.[^10]

**Claim C-07-005:** Truman forced Secretary of the Army Kenneth Royall into retirement for refusing to comply.
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** History.com

Secretary of the Army Kenneth Royall was even more resistant, expressing concern about the order's effect on enlistment and morale, particularly in the South. Truman, who would settle for nothing less than full integration, forced Royall into retirement when he refused to comply.[^11]

The Air Force, by contrast, was pragmatic. It had the newest institutional culture and the most acute personnel needs. The Air Force was effectively integrated by 1949.[^12]

The Army moved more slowly, agreeing to integration in principle by March 1950 but not completing the process for years.[^13]

---

### Shelley v. Kraemer

Beyond executive orders, the Truman administration took unprecedented action in the courts. In *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948), the Supreme Court considered whether racially restrictive housing covenants—deed restrictions that prohibited sale of property to Black buyers—could be legally enforced.

**Claim C-06-007:** The Truman administration filed an amicus brief in Shelley v. Kraemer supporting the unconstitutionality of restrictive covenants.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Supreme Court records

For the first time in a civil rights case, the federal government filed an amicus curiae brief on behalf of the plaintiffs—the Black family seeking to keep a home they had purchased in St. Louis. Solicitor General Philip Perlman argued that the restrictive covenants violated the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause.[^14]

The Supreme Court agreed. In a unanimous 6-0 decision (three justices recused, likely because they owned property with such covenants), Chief Justice Fred Vinson held that while private parties could make discriminatory agreements, state courts could not enforce them. The decision struck a major blow against residential segregation.[^15]

---

### The 1948 Election

The 1948 presidential election was supposed to be unwinnable for Truman. The Democratic Party had split three ways: Dixiecrats to the right, Henry Wallace's Progressive Party to the left. Republican Thomas Dewey led comfortably in polls. Newspapers prepared "DEWEY DEFEATS TRUMAN" headlines.[^16]

**Claim C-07-003:** Thurmond won four states with 39 electoral votes.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Official election records

The Dixiecrats carried four Deep South states—Alabama, Louisiana, Mississippi, and South Carolina—with 39 electoral votes. They had hoped to deny any candidate an Electoral College majority, throwing the election to the House of Representatives where Southern leverage might extract concessions.[^17]

It didn't work. Truman won 303 electoral votes to Dewey's 189 and Thurmond's 39. He carried 49.5% of the popular vote. Despite losing the Deep South, he held enough other Southern states while sweeping the North—including crucial Black votes in cities like Chicago, Cleveland, and Philadelphia.[^18]

The election proved that a Democrat could advocate civil rights and still win. It validated Truman's gamble—or his conviction, or both.

---

### Implementation: Korea and Beyond

The Korean War, which began in June 1950, accelerated military integration.

In combat, segregated units proved inefficient. When the all-Black 24th Infantry Regiment suffered high casualties, replacements were integrated into previously all-white units out of necessity. The practice spread. Commanders discovered that integrated units performed at least as well as segregated ones—often better.[^19]

The last all-Black unit in the U.S. Army—the 24th Infantry, the last of the historic "Buffalo Soldier" regiments—was deactivated on October 1, 1951, its soldiers reassigned to integrated units.[^20]

Full implementation of Executive Order 9981, including the desegregation of military schools, hospitals, and bases, continued under President Eisenhower. The last segregated Army units were not dissolved until September 1954.[^21]

---

### Other Civil Rights Actions

Truman's civil rights efforts extended beyond the headline achievements:

**Fair Employment Practice:** Though Congress refused to create a permanent FEPC, Truman's Executive Order 9980 established fair employment requirements for the federal civilian workforce.[^22]

**Judicial Appointments:** Truman appointed William Hastie as the first Black federal appeals court judge (Third Circuit, 1949), building on his earlier appointment of Hastie as the first Black federal judge (District of the Virgin Islands, 1937, by FDR, upgraded by Truman).[^23]

**Housing:** The administration supported legal challenges to housing discrimination, culminating in the *Shelley v. Kraemer* brief.

**Public Statements:** Truman continued to speak publicly about civil rights, maintaining pressure even as legislative progress remained blocked.

---

### What Was Achieved

By the time Truman left office in January 1953, his civil rights legacy included:

**Accomplished:**
- Military desegregation initiated (completed 1954)
- Federal workforce fair employment established
- First presidential civil rights committee
- First presidential civil rights message to Congress
- First presidential address to NAACP
- Amicus brief in *Shelley v. Kraemer*

**Not Accomplished:**
- No anti-lynching legislation
- No poll tax abolition
- No permanent FEPC
- No comprehensive civil rights act

The gap between ambition and achievement was real. Southern Democratic control of Congress blocked legislation. But the executive actions Truman took were substantial, and his rhetorical leadership established civil rights as a presidential priority for the first time since Reconstruction.

---

### The Nature of Truman's Achievement

Historians debate how to assess Truman's civil rights record.

**The Celebratory View:** Truman was a profile in courage who risked his political career to advance racial justice. He took unprecedented action when Congress refused to act, using executive power creatively to achieve integration.

**The Critical View:** Truman's actions were politically calculated to win Black votes in a tight election. He never abandoned personal prejudices. The gap between rhetoric and legislation shows the limits of his commitment.

**A Balanced Assessment:** Both perspectives capture partial truths. Truman took genuine political risks for civil rights at a time when such action was deeply unpopular. His executive actions had real consequences, particularly military desegregation. At the same time, his private attitudes remained troubling, and his legislative achievements were nil.

Perhaps most importantly, Truman demonstrated that a national politician could champion civil rights and survive—even win. The Dixiecrat revolt failed. The "solid South" cracked but did not bring down the Democratic Party. Future politicians, including John F. Kennedy and Lyndon Johnson, would build on this precedent.

The path from Truman to the Civil Rights Act of 1964 was long and bloody, requiring the leadership of the civil rights movement itself. But Truman helped place the federal government on the side of that struggle, making explicit what had been implicit since the Civil War: the Constitution's promises belonged to all Americans.
