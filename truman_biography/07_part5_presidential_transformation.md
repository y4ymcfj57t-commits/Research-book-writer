# Part V: Presidential Transformation

## Violence, Outrage, and the Turn to Civil Rights (1945-1948)

---

### Chapter Summary

The transformation of Harry Truman from a man who used racial slurs in private correspondence to the president who would desegregate the military began with violence. The brutal attacks on Black veterans returning from World War II—including the blinding of Isaac Woodard and the Moore's Ford lynchings—provoked in Truman a visceral moral outrage that political calculation alone cannot explain. In response, he created the first presidential civil rights committee, delivered a historic address to the NAACP, and sent Congress the first presidential civil rights message since Reconstruction. Whether this constituted a genuine conversion or a compartmentalization of prejudice and principle, the actions were unprecedented and consequential.

---

### An Accidental President

Harry Truman became president on April 12, 1945, under circumstances no one anticipated. Franklin Roosevelt, elected to an unprecedented fourth term just months earlier, died of a cerebral hemorrhage at Warm Springs, Georgia. Truman, who had been vice president for only eighty-two days and had met privately with Roosevelt only twice during that period, was suddenly responsible for ending World War II and shaping the postwar world.[^1]

"I felt like the moon, the stars, and all the planets had fallen on me," Truman told reporters the day after Roosevelt's death.[^2]

In those first chaotic months, civil rights was not on Truman's agenda. The war in Europe ended in May 1945; the atomic bombs fell on Japan in August; surrender came in September. Truman was consumed with demobilization, reconversion to a peacetime economy, and the emerging Cold War with the Soviet Union.

But events would force civil rights onto his desk—events involving men who had fought for their country and returned home to be brutalized for their race.

---

### The Violence of 1946

The year 1946 saw an eruption of racial violence across the South, much of it directed at Black veterans. Men who had fought fascism abroad came home to face terrorism at home. The contrast was not lost on them—or on President Truman.

#### The Columbia, Tennessee, Race Riot (February 1946)

On February 25-26, 1946, violence erupted in Columbia, Tennessee, after a dispute between a Black Navy veteran, James Stephenson, and a white store clerk escalated into a brawl. Stephenson had been a welterweight on the Navy boxing team; when the white clerk struck his mother, Stephenson retaliated, sending the clerk through a plate glass window.[^3]

White mobs began gathering. Black residents of the Mink Slide neighborhood—led by World War II veterans—armed themselves, took up defensive positions, and shot out streetlights. Police and state troopers swept through the Black neighborhood, firing randomly, searching homes without warrants, and confiscating weapons. More than one hundred Black men were arrested.[^4]

The NAACP sent lawyers to defend the accused, including a young Thurgood Marshall, who narrowly escaped a lynching himself when he was pulled over by police on a dark road outside town.[^5]

**Claim C-05-Related:** The Columbia riot reflected a new spirit of resistance among Black veterans.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Tennessee Encyclopedia; multiple historical accounts

#### The Moore's Ford Lynching (July 1946)

On July 25, 1946, near Monroe, Georgia, a white mob murdered four Black Americans in what became known as the Moore's Ford lynching. The victims were George W. Dorsey, a World War II veteran who had served in the Pacific for nearly five years, his wife Mae Murray Dorsey (seven months pregnant), Roger Malcom, and Dorothy Malcom (killed on her twentieth birthday).[^6]

**Claim C-05-002:** On July 25, 1946, a white mob murdered four African Americans at Moore's Ford Bridge in Georgia; George Dorsey was a WWII veteran.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** FBI records; Georgia Historical Society; multiple accounts

A white planter had posted bond for Roger Malcom, who was jailed after allegedly stabbing a white man. As the planter drove the two couples back to his farm, twenty to thirty armed white men stopped the car at Moore's Ford Bridge, dragged out Malcom and Dorsey, tied them to a tree, and shot them repeatedly. When one of the women recognized a member of the mob, both women were killed as well.[^7]

The murders provoked national outrage. President Truman directed the FBI to investigate—the agency's first involvement in a civil rights case of this nature.[^8]

**Claim C-05-003:** Truman directed the FBI to investigate Moore's Ford, marking the agency's first civil rights case involvement.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** FBI records; NPR reporting

No one was ever prosecuted. An all-white grand jury refused to indict. The case remains officially unsolved.[^9]

#### The Blinding of Isaac Woodard (February 1946)

The case that most directly catalyzed Truman's civil rights turn involved Sergeant Isaac Woodard, a Black Army veteran from South Carolina.

**Claim C-05-001:** On February 12, 1946, Isaac Woodard was beaten by South Carolina police and permanently blinded hours after his honorable discharge.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Richard Gergel, *Unexampled Courage*; court records; PBS documentary

On February 12, 1946—only hours after receiving his honorable discharge from the Army—Woodard was traveling home on a Greyhound bus through South Carolina. When he asked the driver if he could use the restroom at a stop, the driver cursed at him. Woodard, still in uniform, responded in kind. The driver contacted police at the next stop in Batesburg, South Carolina.[^10]

The local chief of police, Lynwood Shull, removed Woodard from the bus and took him to a nearby alley. There, Shull and other officers beat Woodard with nightsticks. They arrested him for disorderly conduct and continued beating him in jail. At some point during the assault, Shull rammed his blackjack into Woodard's eye sockets, crushing his eyes and destroying his optic nerves. Isaac Woodard, twenty-six years old, was permanently blinded.[^11]

Three weeks later, Woodard's family found him in an Army hospital, unable to see and uncertain what had happened to him. He had been denied medical treatment for days after the attack.[^12]

---

### "My God! I Had No Idea"

News of these attacks reached President Truman through NAACP executive secretary Walter White, who had been documenting violence against Black veterans. On September 19, 1946, White and a delegation of religious and labor leaders met with Truman in the Oval Office.[^13]

**Claim C-05-004:** Walter White told Truman about Woodard's blinding; Truman exclaimed "My God! I had no idea it was as terrible as that!"
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** Walter White memoir, *A Man Called White*

White recalled the meeting in his memoir:

> When I told him about the blinding of Sergeant Woodard, Truman stood up and exclaimed, "My God! I had no idea it was as terrible as that! We've got to do something!"[^14]

This account comes from a single source—White's own memoir—and must be treated with appropriate caution. White had obvious reasons to portray his role favorably. But the subsequent actions Truman took are consistent with genuine outrage.

**Claim C-05-005:** Truman said his "stomach turned over" when he learned of violence against returning Black soldiers.
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** NPS; multiple secondary citations

Truman later conveyed his alarm in multiple contexts: "My stomach turned over when I learned that Negro soldiers, just back from overseas, were being dumped out of army trucks in Mississippi and beaten. Whatever my inclinations as a native of Missouri might have been, as President I know this is bad. I shall fight to end evils like this."[^15]

That phrasing—"whatever my inclinations as a native of Missouri might have been"—is remarkable. Truman acknowledges his racist heritage while asserting that presidential duty requires him to act against it. The prejudices remain; the actions transcend them.

**Claim C-05-006:** Truman was "appalled especially" by the Woodard case.
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** Washington Post; multiple accounts

The Woodard case particularly affected Truman. Perhaps it was the symbolism of a soldier in uniform, still wearing the country's cloth, being mutilated by local police. Perhaps it was the specific cruelty of blinding—destroying forever a man's ability to see. Whatever the reason, Truman ordered the Justice Department to prosecute.[^16]

The trial, held in federal court in South Carolina, was a travesty. Chief Shull's defense attorney referred to Woodard as a "nigger" in open court. The all-white jury deliberated thirty minutes before acquitting Shull. The judge congratulated them on their verdict.[^17]

### The President's Committee on Civil Rights

Truman's response to the violence of 1946 was institutional. On December 5, 1946, he signed Executive Order 9808, creating the President's Committee on Civil Rights—the first presidential civil rights commission in American history.[^18]

**Claim C-06-001:** On December 5, 1946, Truman signed Executive Order 9808 establishing the President's Committee on Civil Rights.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Executive Order 9808; Federal Register

The Committee, chaired by Charles E. Wilson (president of General Electric), included fifteen distinguished members from business, labor, religion, and academia. Truman charged them with examining "the condition of civil rights in the country" and recommending "more adequate means and procedures for the protection of the civil rights of the people of the United States."[^19]

The Committee worked for nearly a year. On October 29, 1947, it delivered a 178-page report titled *To Secure These Rights*—a reference to the Declaration of Independence's assertion that governments are instituted to secure people's rights.[^20]

**Claim C-06-002:** The Committee produced a 178-page report titled "To Secure These Rights" with 34 recommendations.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** The report itself; Truman Library

The report was devastating in its assessment of American race relations. It documented discrimination in voting, employment, housing, education, and public accommodations. It catalogued the violence—lynchings, beatings, police brutality—that enforced racial hierarchy. And it proposed thirty-four recommendations for federal action, including:

- Federal anti-lynching legislation
- Abolition of poll taxes
- A permanent Fair Employment Practice Commission
- Desegregation of the armed forces
- A permanent Civil Rights Commission
- A Civil Rights Division in the Department of Justice[^21]

The report attacked segregation head-on—seven years before the Supreme Court would do so in *Brown v. Board of Education*. It laid out an agenda that would take two decades to enact but that set the course for the civil rights movement.[^22]

---

### The NAACP Address: "All Americans"

Before the Committee's report was released, Truman made another historic gesture. On June 29, 1947, he became the first American president to address the NAACP, speaking to the organization's 38th annual conference at the Lincoln Memorial.[^23]

**Claim C-06-003:** On June 29, 1947, Truman became the first American president to address the NAACP.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Multiple accounts; Truman Library

The setting was laden with symbolism. Truman spoke from the steps of the memorial to the president who had freed the enslaved—a president whom Truman's mother had taught him to despise. Eleanor Roosevelt sat on the dais. The speech was broadcast to a national radio audience.[^24]

**Claim C-06-004:** Truman declared at the NAACP speech: "When I say all Americans, I mean all Americans."
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Speech transcript; multiple recordings

Truman's address was unprecedented in its directness:

> "I should like to talk to you briefly about civil rights and human freedom. It is my deep conviction that we have reached a turning point in the long history of our country's efforts to guarantee freedom and equality to all our citizens... Many of our people still suffer the indignity of insult, the harrowing fear of intimidation, and, I regret to say, the threat of physical injury and mob violence. The prejudice and intolerance in which these evils are rooted still exist. The conscience of our nation, and the legal machinery which enforces it, have not yet secured to each citizen full freedom from fear."[^25]

And then the key declaration: "When I say all Americans, I mean all Americans."[^26]

No president since Lincoln had spoken so forcefully about civil rights. No president before Truman had addressed the NAACP. The speech mattered—and Truman knew it would cost him.

At the time, roughly 80 percent of Americans opposed Truman's civil rights agenda.[^27] Southern Democrats were already restive. The speech was an act of political courage that defied electoral logic—unless Truman was calculating that Black votes in Northern cities could offset Southern losses.

Both may be true. Courage and calculation are not mutually exclusive.

---

### The Civil Rights Message to Congress

The Committee's report arrived in October 1947. Truman embraced it fully.

On February 2, 1948, he sent Congress a special message on civil rights—the first presidential civil rights message since Reconstruction. Based on *To Secure These Rights*, it proposed a ten-point program including:

- Anti-lynching legislation
- Protection of voting rights
- A permanent Fair Employment Practice Commission
- Prohibition of discrimination in interstate transportation
- Home rule for the District of Columbia
- Statehood for Hawaii and Alaska
- Claims settlement for Japanese Americans interned during WWII[^28]

**Claim C-06-005:** On February 2, 1948, Truman delivered the first presidential civil rights message to Congress.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Congressional Record; Truman Library

The message was explosive. Southern senators immediately threatened filibuster. Senator Richard Russell of Georgia called it "a vicious and unwarranted attack on our Southern institutions."[^29]

Truman had known this would happen. He had made his choice.

---

### Was It Genuine?

The central interpretive question is whether Truman's civil rights turn represented genuine moral conviction, cynical political calculation, or some combination.

**Evidence for Genuine Conviction:**
- The visceral reaction to Isaac Woodard's blinding, documented by multiple witnesses
- The willingness to take positions opposed by 80 percent of Americans
- The personal risk—Southern Democrats controlled key congressional committees
- Truman's own words: "Whatever my inclinations as a native of Missouri might have been, as President I know this is bad"

**Evidence for Political Calculation:**
- The timing: 1948 was an election year; Truman needed Black votes
- The FEPC failure of 1946 had already demonstrated the electoral importance of civil rights
- Cold War considerations: America's racial violence was propaganda gold for the Soviet Union
- Truman never fully abandoned private racial prejudices

**The Synthesis:**
The evidence supports a complex picture. Truman was genuinely outraged by violence against Black veterans—men who had served their country and been brutalized for their race. This outrage aligned with his sense of military honor and fair dealing. At the same time, he understood the political landscape: Black voters were becoming crucial to Democratic success in Northern states.

Multiple motivations can operate simultaneously. The moral and the political reinforced each other. What matters historically is that Truman acted—taking risks that a purely cynical politician would have avoided, while also benefiting politically from positions he took on principle.

The man who used racial slurs in private letters became the president who demanded civil rights legislation in public messages. Both things are true. The paradox is not resolved but lived.
