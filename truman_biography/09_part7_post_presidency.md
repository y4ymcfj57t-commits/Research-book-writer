# Part VII: Post-Presidency and Final Assessment

## The Persistence of Prejudice (1953-1972)

---

### Chapter Summary

Harry Truman's post-presidency reveals the limits of his transformation. While he had taken unprecedented executive action for civil rights, his private racial prejudices persisted. In 1960, he publicly condemned the sit-in movement, linking it to communism and declaring that "the Negro should behave himself." Martin Luther King Jr., who had admired Truman's presidential record, wrote expressing his confusion and disappointment. Truman continued to use racial slurs in private conversation until his death in 1972. The final assessment of Truman's legacy requires grappling with this paradox: genuine achievement coexisting with genuine prejudice.

---

### Return to Independence

When Truman left the White House in January 1953, he returned to Independence, Missouri—the town where he had grown up, the place that had shaped his racial attitudes. He was 68 years old.

Unlike modern ex-presidents, Truman had no presidential pension (Congress would later enact one partly because of his financial difficulties), no Secret Service protection, no presidential library immediately available. He lived modestly in his wife's family home, wrote his memoirs, and remained engaged in Democratic politics.[^1]

The civil rights movement was entering a new phase. *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) struck down school segregation—vindicating the arguments Truman's civil rights committee had made seven years earlier. The Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955-1956) launched Martin Luther King Jr. to national prominence. Across the South, Black Americans were organizing, protesting, demanding the rights that federal law now guaranteed but local custom denied.

Truman watched these developments from Missouri—sometimes supportively, sometimes not.

---

### The Sit-In Controversy (1960)

On February 1, 1960, four Black college students sat down at a segregated Woolworth's lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina, and refused to leave until served. The sit-in movement spread across the South with remarkable speed, as young people challenged segregation through nonviolent direct action.[^2]

Harry Truman's response shocked those who remembered his civil rights record.

**Claim C-08-001:** In 1960, Truman made "defamatory statements linking the sit-in demonstrations with communism."
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** King Institute Encyclopedia

**Claim C-08-002:** Truman said: "If anyone came into my store and tried to stop business I'd throw him out. The Negro should behave himself."
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** King Institute Encyclopedia

Truman publicly condemned the sit-ins, suggesting they were communist-influenced and defending the property rights of business owners. "If anyone came into my store and tried to stop business I'd throw him out," he declared. "The Negro should behave himself and show he's a good citizen."[^3]

The language was revealing. Not "citizens should" but "the Negro should"—as if Black Americans constituted a special category requiring good behavior to earn rights that white Americans possessed by default.

**Claim C-08-003:** Martin Luther King Jr. wrote to Truman expressing "confusion and disappointment."
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** King Institute Encyclopedia

Martin Luther King Jr., who had admired Truman's presidential civil rights record, wrote him a letter expressing his "confusion and disappointment" at these statements. King acknowledged Truman's historic achievements but could not reconcile them with his opposition to peaceful protest.[^4]

The exchange illuminated something important about Truman's civil rights legacy. His presidential actions had been driven by constitutional principle, outrage at violence, and political calculation—not by any fundamental revision of his racial attitudes. When Black protest took forms he found disruptive or presumptuous, his underlying prejudices resurfaced.

---

### Private Racism to the End

**Claim C-08-004:** Truman "continued to use racial slurs in private conversation for the rest of his life."
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** Seattle Times, November 3, 1991; various accounts

According to historians and journalists who studied his private life, Truman continued to use racial slurs in private conversation until his death.[^5] The man who had signed Executive Order 9981 still said "nigger" among friends and family.

This was not merely an old man clinging to the language of his youth. It reflected a genuine persistence of the attitudes documented in his 1911 letter to Bess Wallace. Whatever transformation Truman underwent was partial at best. He learned to act against his prejudices when duty and circumstances required it, but he never fully abandoned the prejudices themselves.

Truman's racial attitudes in retirement included:
- Opposition to sit-ins and direct action protests
- Use of racial slurs in private settings
- Discomfort with interracial marriage
- Characterization of Martin Luther King Jr. as a "troublemaker"[^6]

---

### Death and Legacy

Harry S. Truman died on December 26, 1972, in Kansas City, Missouri, at age 88. He was buried at the Truman Library in Independence.

By the time of his death, the civil rights movement had achieved much of what his 1947 committee had recommended:
- The Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited discrimination in public accommodations and employment
- The Voting Rights Act of 1965 protected Black voting rights
- The Fair Housing Act of 1968 addressed housing discrimination

Truman's role in this story was foundational but limited. He had placed the presidency behind civil rights. He had demonstrated that a national politician could advocate for racial justice and survive. He had desegregated the military through executive action. He had set an agenda that subsequent administrations and the civil rights movement itself would ultimately achieve.

But he had not overcome his own prejudices. The trajectory from 1911 to 1972 was not a conversion narrative but something more complex and more human.

---

## Final Assessment: A Complex Legacy

### The Case for Truman as Civil Rights Hero

1. **Unprecedented Action:** Truman was the first president since Reconstruction to make civil rights a federal priority. His executive orders, committee, congressional message, and NAACP speech were genuine firsts.

2. **Real Consequences:** Military desegregation affected millions of people. Black soldiers served and advanced in integrated units. The armed forces became one of America's most integrated institutions.

3. **Political Risk:** Truman took these actions at substantial political cost. The Dixiecrat revolt was real. He could have lost the 1948 election. He chose principle over the path of least resistance.

4. **Setting the Agenda:** *To Secure These Rights* outlined the civil rights agenda for the next two decades. Truman made these issues inescapable for subsequent administrations.

5. **Moral Framework:** Truman articulated civil rights as a constitutional and moral imperative, not merely a political preference. His framing shaped how the issue would be debated.

### The Case Against Truman as Civil Rights Hero

1. **Persistent Personal Racism:** Truman held racist attitudes throughout his life, expressed them in private correspondence, and never repudiated them.

2. **The 1935 Anti-Lynching Vote:** As a senator, Truman participated in killing legislation that could have saved lives. This vote contradicts any simple narrative of consistent principle.

3. **Political Calculation:** Much of Truman's civil rights action coincided with electoral necessity. Black votes were crucial in 1948. Self-interest and principle aligned.

4. **Limited Achievement:** Truman achieved nothing legislatively. Congress blocked every civil rights proposal. The gap between rhetoric and results was vast.

5. **Post-Presidential Backsliding:** Truman's opposition to sit-ins and continued use of slurs suggest his transformation was strategic rather than sincere.

### A Synthesis: Neither Hero nor Hypocrite

The evidence does not support either a purely celebratory or purely condemnatory assessment. Truman's legacy is genuinely paradoxical:

**He held racist views AND took anti-racist actions.**
**He used slurs in private AND demanded equal rights in public.**
**He calculated politically AND acted on principle.**
**He voted against anti-lynching legislation AND created the first civil rights committee.**

These contradictions are not resolved by choosing one side or the other. They coexisted in the same person. Understanding Truman requires holding both in view simultaneously.

Several interpretive frameworks may help:

**1. Constitutional Duty vs. Personal Prejudice:** Truman developed a robust sense that presidential duty required upholding constitutional principles regardless of personal feelings. The office demanded more than the man naturally gave. "Whatever my inclinations as a native of Missouri might have been, as President I know this is bad."

**2. Violence as Catalyst:** The brutalization of Black veterans in 1946 appears to have genuinely shocked Truman in ways that abstractions never did. Isaac Woodard's blinding was not a statistic but an atrocity that violated Truman's sense of military honor and fair dealing.

**3. Political Learning:** Truman's Pendergast education taught him that Black votes mattered. His 1940 campaign taught him that inclusive rhetoric could work. Political experience created incentives to act against his prejudices.

**4. Generational Limits:** Truman was born in 1884 and raised by Confederate sympathizers. He evolved further than most white men of his generation and background—but evolution is not conversion. He never fully escaped his origins.

---

### The Meaning for History

Truman's story illuminates something important about how social change happens.

Progress does not require that individuals fully overcome their prejudices. It requires that they act against them when it matters. Truman the private racist became Truman the public civil rights advocate not because he stopped being racist but because he found reasons—constitutional, political, moral—to act as if he had.

This is both less inspiring and more realistic than a conversion narrative. It suggests that social change can proceed through people who are themselves imperfect vehicles. The civil rights movement did not wait for white Americans to become enlightened; it created conditions where acting rightly became possible for those who still thought wrongly.

Truman's legacy is thus double-edged. He demonstrated what was possible—a president using executive power for civil rights despite overwhelming opposition. He also demonstrated what was persistent—racist attitudes that even unprecedented action could not erase.

The grandson of slaveholders issued Executive Order 9981.
The man who wrote of "niggers" addressed the NAACP.
The senator who helped kill anti-lynching legislation created the first civil rights commission.
The president who desegregated the military condemned sit-ins as communist-inspired.

All of these things are true. Harry S. Truman contains them all.

## A Note on Historical Method

This biographical study has attempted to apply rigorous evidentiary standards to a contested legacy:

- **Documented claims:** Where primary sources exist (letters, official documents, speeches), claims are stated with confidence.
- **Interpreted claims:** Where evidence requires interpretation, multiple perspectives are presented.
- **Uncertain claims:** Where sources are limited or contested, uncertainty is acknowledged.
- **Moral complexity:** Neither hagiography nor condemnation, but honest engagement with contradiction.

The goal has been to present Harry Truman's relationship to slavery, racism, and civil rights with the complexity the evidence demands. Readers may draw their own conclusions about how to weigh his achievements against his failures, his public actions against his private attitudes.

What the evidence does not support is simplification in either direction. Truman was not simply a civil rights hero who overcame his background. He was not simply a racist whose public actions were cynical calculation. He was a human being whose life encompassed both genuine prejudice and genuine achievement—a paradox that reflects the larger paradox of American history itself.
