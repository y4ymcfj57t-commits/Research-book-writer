# Part III: Political Rise and the Pendergast Era

## Machine Politics and Black Voters (1919-1934)

---

### Chapter Summary

Harry Truman's entry into politics through the Pendergast machine in Kansas City introduced him to a world where Black votes mattered. While his personal racial views remained unchanged, his political calculations began to evolve. The machine's pragmatic inclusion of Black constituents—unusual for Democrats outside Northern cities—planted seeds that would later bear fruit. This period also included Truman's brief, revealing encounter with the Ku Klux Klan.

---

### Return from War and Entry into Politics

Harry Truman returned from World War I in 1919 as a thirty-five-year-old captain who had commanded Battery D of the 129th Field Artillery in France. He had proven himself under fire, earned the respect of his men—many of them Irish Catholics from Kansas City—and developed leadership skills that would serve him in politics.[^1]

After a failed venture running a haberdashery in Kansas City with his Jewish friend and army comrade Eddie Jacobson, Truman turned to politics. His entry point was the Pendergast machine, the Democratic political organization that dominated Kansas City and much of Jackson County, Missouri.[^2]

The Pendergast connection came through Truman's Army service. Jim Pendergast, nephew of boss Tom Pendergast, had served with Truman in France. When Truman sought political opportunity, the Pendergast organization was willing to back him.[^3]

---

### The Pendergast Machine and Black Voters

The Pendergast machine's approach to Black voters was distinctive for a Democratic organization in a border state. Unlike the solid Democratic South, where Black citizens were systematically disenfranchised, Kansas City's Democrats actively courted Black support.

**Claim C-03-005:** The Pendergast machine attracted "considerable black support" when Democrats elsewhere excluded Black voters.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Grothaus, "Kansas City Blacks, Harry Truman and the Pendergast Machine"; Pendergast KC Project

One of the defining aspects of "Boss" Tom Pendergast's machine politics was its approach to African American voters. During the early twentieth century, at a time when Black people were routinely excluded from the vote by Democratic regimes in most of the former slave South, Pendergast's Democratic organization in Kansas City succeeded in part by attracting considerable Black support.[^4]

This was not racial liberalism but pragmatic politics. Black votes were "desperately needed in the early 1920s, as the Democrats sank to one of their lowest ebbs of the 20th century."[^5] The machine offered patronage, protection, and a degree of political recognition in exchange for reliable votes.

Dr. William J. Thompkins, an African American physician and Democrat, worked to organize Black voters while maintaining their independence as a faction that "all the white factions had to court."[^6] Chester Arthur Franklin, editor of the Black newspaper *The Call*, though a Republican, sometimes endorsed Pendergast-backed candidates—including, later, Harry Truman.[^7]

This political education would prove formative for Truman. He learned that Black votes could swing elections, that Black leaders expected tangible benefits for their support, and that a Democrat could win Black backing through practical deliverables rather than ideological transformation.

---

### County Judge: First Political Office

In 1922, with Pendergast backing, Truman won election as eastern judge of Jackson County—an administrative rather than judicial position, equivalent to a county commissioner. He was responsible for roads, public buildings, and county institutions.[^8]

**Claim C-03-004:** As Jackson County Judge, Truman supported funding for Kansas City's Black public hospital and county home for orphaned African American children.
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** NPS, "Harry S Truman and Civil Rights"

In this role, Truman supported funding for institutions serving Black constituents: Kansas City's Black public hospital and the county home for orphaned African American children.[^9] These were not revolutionary acts—they were the ordinary business of a machine politician keeping faith with constituents. But they marked a departure from the rural Missouri world where Truman had formed his racial views.

Truman lost his bid for reelection in 1924, in part because of factional Democratic divisions and Ku Klux Klan opposition. He returned to win the position of presiding judge (the county's chief executive) in 1926 and served two terms until 1934.[^10]

---

### The Ku Klux Klan Episode

One of the most revealing incidents of Truman's early political career was his brief flirtation with the Ku Klux Klan in 1922.

The 1920s Klan was at the height of its second-wave power, claiming millions of members nationwide. Unlike the Reconstruction-era Klan, this organization combined white supremacy with nativism—targeting Catholics, Jews, and immigrants as well as Black Americans. In states like Indiana, the Klan essentially controlled the government. In Missouri, it was a significant political force.[^11]

**Claim C-03-001:** In 1922, Truman paid $10 to a KKK organizer and may have signed a membership card.
**Confidence:** CONTESTED
**Sources:** McCullough, *Truman*; multiple secondary accounts

In the summer of 1922, with Truman running for county judge, a political ally advised him to join the Klan—"on the grounds that it was 'good politics.'"[^12] Two candidates already in the race had Klan support. Truman, seeking any advantage in a competitive primary, was receptive.

He paid the $10 membership fee to a Klan organizer. Some accounts suggest he may have signed a membership card or even taken an oath.[^13] The full extent of his involvement is contested.

**Claim C-03-002:** Truman demanded his money back after being told he could not hire Catholics.
**Confidence:** PROBABLE
**Sources:** McCullough, *Truman*; JCHS Journal

What is clear is that the relationship ended quickly. When Klan representatives told Truman he could not, as a county official, hire Catholics, he balked. His Battery D men—the soldiers he had led in France—included Irish Catholics. He was not about to betray "his boys."[^14]

Truman summoned the Klan messenger, renounced any membership, and demanded his ten dollars back. According to accounts, he got it.[^15] The Klan became his enemy; his life was reportedly threatened during the 1924 campaign.[^16]

**Claim C-03-003:** Truman had commanded a predominantly Catholic artillery battery (Battery D) in WWI.
**Confidence:** VERIFIED
**Sources:** Military records; McCullough, *Truman*

---

### Interpreting the Klan Episode

How should we understand Truman's Klan flirtation?

**The Sympathetic Interpretation:** Truman was a naive political novice who briefly considered joining what he thought was a "patriotic organization," then rejected it when he learned of its anti-Catholic bigotry. His rejection showed the limits of his prejudice—he would not betray personal loyalty for political advantage.

**The Critical Interpretation:** Truman was willing to join an organization known primarily for white supremacy. His objection was not to racism but to anti-Catholicism, which threatened people he knew personally. This suggests his rejection of the Klan was about loyalty to friends, not opposition to bigotry.

**The Documentary Reality:** Both interpretations capture something true. Truman's racism was real, but so was his sense of personal honor. The Klan episode reveals a man whose prejudices were genuine but whose character included countervailing values—loyalty, constitutionalism, fair dealing—that would later prove more decisive.

Historians generally agree that Truman was never formally initiated, never attended a Klan meeting, and never claimed membership afterward. His close friendship with Eddie Jacobson (Jewish) and his loyalty to Catholic army comrades suggest genuine boundaries to his prejudices—even as his anti-Black attitudes remained intact.

---

### Political Lessons Learned

Truman's years in Jackson County politics taught him several lessons relevant to his later civil rights actions:

**1. Coalition Politics:** Success required assembling diverse coalitions. The Pendergast machine showed that Democrats could win Black votes through practical politics, not ideological appeals.

**2. The Value of Loyalty:** Truman's political identity centered on personal loyalty—to Pendergast, to his army comrades, to his constituents. This value would later extend to Black veterans whose abuse offended his sense of honor.

**3. Practical Governance:** As county judge, Truman gained experience in public administration. He built roads, managed budgets, and dealt with competing interests. This competence-focused approach would characterize his civil rights executive orders—practical measures rather than grand rhetoric.

**4. Electoral Vulnerability:** Truman learned that elections could be lost. His 1924 defeat, partly due to Klan opposition, taught him that standing up to bigotry had costs—and that he could survive them.

---

### The Road to the Senate

In 1934, Tom Pendergast needed a candidate for U.S. Senate. His first choices declined. Truman, fifty years old and looking for advancement beyond county politics, was available and acceptable.

Pendergast backed Truman for the Democratic nomination. With machine support, Truman won the primary and, in the Democratic landslide of 1934, the general election. He arrived in Washington in January 1935 as the junior senator from Missouri—and, in the eyes of many, "the Senator from Pendergast."[^17]

Truman's racial views at this point remained substantially unchanged from his 1911 letter. He was a machine politician who understood that Black votes mattered in Kansas City but who harbored the prejudices of his upbringing. His private correspondence continued to include racial slurs.

The Senate years would see Truman begin a more complex evolution—or at least a more complex positioning—on racial issues. The Pendergast connection had taught him that Black constituents deserved practical attention. The Senate would teach him something about the limits of that pragmatism when federal civil rights legislation came to a vote.
