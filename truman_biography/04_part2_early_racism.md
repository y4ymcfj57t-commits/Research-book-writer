# Part II: Early Racism and Formation

## Documented Prejudice (1884-1918)

---

### Chapter Summary

The young Harry Truman expressed virulent racist views in his private correspondence, views that aligned perfectly with his Confederate-sympathizing upbringing. The documentary record from this period—particularly his letters to his future wife Bess Wallace—provides unambiguous evidence of white supremacist beliefs, including explicit statements about racial hierarchy that he held as a young man of twenty-seven.

---

### The 1911 Letter: Primary Evidence

On June 22, 1911, Harry Truman was a twenty-seven-year-old farmer working his family's land near Grandview, Missouri. He was courting Bess Wallace, the woman he would marry eight years later. In a letter to her that day, he wrote candidly about his racial views:

> "I think one man is just as good as another so long as he's honest and decent and not a nigger or a Chinaman. Uncle Will says that the Lord made a white man from dust, a nigger from mud, then He threw up what was left and it came down a Chinaman. He does hate Chinese and Japs. So do I. It is race prejudice I guess. But I am strongly of the opinion that negroes ought to be in Africa, yellow men in Asia, and white men in Europe and America."[^1]

**Sources:** Original letter, National Archives Catalog ID 157638923; Robert Ferrell, ed., *Dear Bess: The Letters from Harry to Bess Truman, 1910-1959* (1983), p. 39

This letter is the single most important document for understanding Truman's early racial attitudes. Several elements deserve careful analysis:

**1. The Casual Tone:** Truman is not making an argument or defending a position. He writes as if stating obvious facts that Bess would naturally share. This suggests these views were unremarkable in his social world.

**2. The Explicit Hierarchy:** Truman articulates a clear racial ranking: whites at the top, Blacks in the middle ("made from mud"), Chinese at the bottom ("what was left"). This is not mere prejudice but a cosmological ordering of humanity.

**3. The Separatist Vision:** His view that each race should remain on its own continent reflects the "colonization" ideology that had attracted some nineteenth-century figures—the belief that racial separation was both natural and necessary.

**4. The Self-Awareness:** Truman acknowledges "It is race prejudice I guess," showing some awareness that his views could be labeled as such, yet he feels no apparent need to qualify or apologize.

**5. Family Transmission:** The reference to "Uncle Will" shows how these attitudes were transmitted within the family, passed down as folk wisdom.

---

### Context: White Supremacy in Early Twentieth-Century Missouri

Truman's 1911 views were not unusual for white Missourians of his time and place—a fact that explains but does not excuse them.

The early twentieth century was the nadir of American race relations. Lynching remained endemic across the South and border states. The Supreme Court's 1896 *Plessy v. Ferguson* decision had enshrined "separate but equal" as constitutional doctrine. Scientific racism—the pseudo-scholarly belief in biological racial hierarchy—was taught in universities and accepted by mainstream intellectuals.[^2]

Missouri in 1911 was a thoroughly segregated society. Black and white children attended separate schools. Public accommodations were divided by race. Interracial marriage was illegal. The Ku Klux Klan would soon experience a massive national revival, with Missouri as one of its strongholds.[^3]

In this context, Truman's letter reflects views that were:
- **Conventional** among white Missourians of his class and region
- **Explicit** in a way that might have been more guarded in public settings
- **Sincere** rather than performative—this was a private letter to an intimate

The historical context does not excuse the views, but it helps explain why Truman felt comfortable expressing them and why his later evolution—partial as it was—represents genuine movement.

---

### Truman's Early Formation

Harry Truman's first three decades shaped the man who would later become president. Several factors reinforced his racial attitudes:

**Education:** Truman graduated from Independence High School in 1901 but never attended college. His formal education did not expose him to perspectives that might have challenged his inherited views. His intellectual formation was largely autodidactic, based on reading history—particularly military history—that reinforced conventional narratives.[^4]

**Work Experience:** Truman worked a variety of jobs—mailroom clerk, timekeeper for a railroad construction crew, bank clerk, farmer—all in predominantly white environments. He had little contact with Black Americans as equals or colleagues.[^5]

**Military Service:** Truman's service in World War I (1917-1918) as a captain of artillery would later prove significant for his racial evolution, but not primarily because of racial contact. His Battery D included Irish Catholics whose friendship would later cause him to reject the Ku Klux Klan. But the segregated Army offered no challenge to his racial assumptions.[^6]

**Social Circle:** Independence and Kansas City society was rigidly divided. The Trumans and Wallaces moved in white middle-class circles where Black Americans appeared, if at all, only as servants.

---

### What the Early Truman Believed

Based on the documentary evidence, the young Harry Truman held the following views:

**1. Biological Racial Hierarchy:** He believed whites were inherently superior to other races, with Blacks and Asians ranked below.

**2. Racial Separation:** He believed each race should occupy its own geographic space—a view that would later be called "separate but equal" at best, or deportation/colonization at worst.

**3. White Entitlement:** He assumed that America and Europe were properly white domains where other races had no legitimate claim to equality.

**4. Casual Dehumanization:** His use of slurs ("nigger," "Chinaman") and crude origin stories reflects a willingness to dehumanize that was socially acceptable in his milieu.

These views represent textbook white supremacy of the early twentieth century. They were the views of millions of white Americans, but they were Truman's views too, held sincerely and expressed without embarrassment.

---

### The Gap Between 1911 and Civil Rights

The distance between the man who wrote the 1911 letter and the president who would desegregate the military in 1948 is vast. How do we understand this trajectory?

Several factors would contribute to Truman's evolution:

1. **Urban Political Experience:** Working within the Pendergast machine in Kansas City would expose Truman to Black voters as a political constituency that mattered.

2. **World War I:** Military service, while not changing his racial views directly, instilled a constitutional patriotism and sense of duty that would later transcend his prejudices.

3. **Political Ambition:** Truman's rise through Democratic politics required appealing to constituencies beyond rural white Missouri.

4. **Exposure to Violence:** The brutal attacks on Black veterans in 1946 would affect Truman viscerally in ways that statistics never could.

5. **Constitutional Understanding:** Truman developed a robust sense that the Constitution applied to all Americans—even as his personal prejudices persisted.

But these factors lay in the future. In 1911, Harry Truman was a white supremacist farmer who saw no contradiction between believing in democracy and believing in racial hierarchy. The two ideas coexisted comfortably, as they did for millions of his contemporaries.

---

### The Persistence of Private Racism

A crucial point often overlooked in accounts of Truman's "conversion": the 1911 letter was not an aberration that he later repudiated. Racist language continued to appear in Truman's private correspondence for decades:

- **1937:** As a U.S. Senator, Truman described White House waiters as "an army of coons" in a letter to his daughter Margaret.[^7]

- **1939:** In a letter to Bess, he referred to "nigger picnic day."[^8]

- **Post-presidency:** According to historians, "he continued to use racial slurs in private conversation for the rest of his life."[^9]

This persistence matters for understanding Truman's legacy. His civil rights actions as president did not emerge from an abandonment of personal prejudice. Rather, they represented the triumph of other values—constitutional duty, political calculation, visceral moral outrage at violence—over prejudices he never fully shed.

The Truman who desegregated the military was still, in private moments, the same man who used racial slurs. This paradox is central to his story and to the story of American racial progress more broadly.

---

### Interpretive Framework: Neither Hero nor Villain

How should we assess the early Truman?

**The Minimizing View:** Some accounts downplay Truman's early racism as merely conventional for his time, emphasizing his later evolution. This approach risks excusing genuine white supremacy and understating what he had to overcome.

**The Condemning View:** Other accounts emphasize Truman's racism to the exclusion of his later achievements, suggesting his civil rights actions were purely cynical. This approach fails to account for the genuine risks he took and the real changes he enacted.

**A More Complete View:** The evidence supports a complex assessment:

1. Truman's early racism was genuine, deeply held, and conventionally expressed.
2. His racist views persisted privately even as his public positions evolved.
3. His civil rights actions were real and consequential, regardless of his private attitudes.
4. The gap between private prejudice and public action reveals something important about how social change happens—often through people who act better than they believe.

The young Harry Truman was a product of his time and place—but he was also responsible for his views. He was not compelled to write what he wrote in 1911. The fact that his views were conventional does not make them any less his own.

What makes Truman's story historically significant is not that he overcame his prejudices—the evidence suggests he never fully did—but that he acted against them when it mattered. The question of how and why he did so is the subject of the chapters that follow.
